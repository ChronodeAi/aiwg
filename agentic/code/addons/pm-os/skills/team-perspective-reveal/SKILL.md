# Show the PM themselves from the outside

Read `knowledge/Resources/pm-excellence-clusters.md` first — it was written by designers and developers, and you are about to generate what *they* would say about this PM.

Self-reporting on abstract virtues is unreliable. A specific voice saying "she champions the why but I never know what we're actually building next" lands where "improve your communication" does not.

## Step 1 — Run the behavioral interview

Frame it: *"8 questions about how you actually work — not how you aspire to work, how you work right now."* Ask one at a time, waiting for each answer.

1. **Decisions** — "When you make a product decision, how do you communicate it? Walk me through what that actually looks like."
2. **Pushback** — "The last time an engineer or designer pushed back on something you wanted — what happened?"
3. **Priorities** — "If I asked one of your engineers right now what they're building and why, what would they say?"
4. **Involvement** — "How early do you get engineers and designers involved when scoping something new?"
5. **Pressure** — "When leadership puts pressure on you, how does that usually land with your team?"
6. **Credit** — "Your last successful launch — who got the credit internally?"
7. **Unglamorous work** — "What tedious or admin-heavy thing do you regularly do that most PMs skip?"
8. **People** — "Who on your team have you put extra effort into recently? What did that look like?"

Let them skip or say "I don't know" — note which questions they avoid or struggle with; those are signals in their own right.

Done when all 8 are asked and you've noted every skipped, vague, or laboured answer.

## Step 2 — Write the two peer responses

Two first-person responses, 150-250 words each, in the candid voice of a colleague answering *"What stands out about this PM? What do they do uniquely well? Where do they fall short?"*

- **The designer** cares about autonomy, design quality, being treated as an expert, communication clarity, not being micromanaged on taste.
- **The engineer** cares about being involved early, clear requirements, technical decisions being respected, not being surprised by scope changes, organizational clarity.

Invent plausible specifics from what the PM told you — "she'd message us before the weekly sync with her read on priorities" beats "she communicates well." Mirror the source material's precision rather than a performance-review register. Each response carries both strengths and gaps.

Done when both responses are specific enough that a real teammate could have written them, and neither reads as uniformly positive or uniformly critical.

## Step 3 — Present and find the gap

Present both, then ask: *"Does any of this surprise you? Which part feels most off from how you'd have described yourself?"*

Read the reaction:
- Surprise at a negative → that's the real blind spot
- Disagreement with a positive → imposter syndrome or genuine overstatement; probe either
- "Yes, exactly right" with no further reflection → push: *"And how do you feel about that?"*

If both responses match what they expected, find the assumption they hold most confidently and test that one hardest.

Then name it: the gap, what it costs them with the team, and one thing they could do this week that would change the perception.

Done when the gap is named with its cost and a single action dated to this week.

## Output

Coaching is cross-project. Save to `📂 Context/Work/Coaching/YYMMDD-team-perspective-reveal.md`, creating the folder if it does not exist.

Offer `situation-retrospective` to ground the outside-in read in a real recent event.
