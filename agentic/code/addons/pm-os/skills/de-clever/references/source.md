# Source: Shreyas Doshi — "Sound Less Clever" (post + analysis chat)

Preserved for provenance. The de-clever skill was compiled from this material via the
skill-extractor pipeline, June 2026.

## The post

> If you're already very good at product, you can immediately get better at product with this
> one weird trick: try to sound less clever when thinking about & discussing product insights
> and ideas.
>
> This forces you to deal at the level of truth rather than clever proxies.
>
> This is easier said than done, but definitely can be done.
>
> Now, in 9 out of 10 companies, once you've made a good decision by thinking and discussing
> without feeling clever or sounding clever, by all means be super-clever and strategically
> abstruse as you present your proposal to other clever people and be sure to make them all
> feel very clever as you do this, whereupon they'll agree and approve your proposal in record
> time, each individual patting themselves on the back for their unique brand of cleverness.

## Key points from the analysis chat (Shreyas + Claude)

### The two claims

- **Epistemic claim:** cleverness is a contaminant in the thinking phase. Trying to sound
  clever — even to yourself — optimizes the proxy (feels sophisticated, surprising, quotable)
  rather than the target (is this true?). Flattening your language strips the rhetorical cover
  and exposes whether anything is actually there. A debugging technique for self-deception.
  Sharper than "if you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it": the pursuit of
  cleverness *actively corrupts* the thinking.
- **Political claim:** at the approval stage, organizations reward cleverness-signaling, not
  truth-seeking. The presentation manufactures a feeling of co-discovered brilliance in the
  approvers; "strategically abstruse" lets each listener fill the gap and feel smart for it.
  "9 out of 10 companies" quietly implies the tenth exists.

### The leakage failure mode and the standing countermeasure

- Claude's push: the two phases aren't cleanly separable in practice. People who get good at
  the phase-two performance find it bleeding back into phase one — the abstruse framing built
  for the room becomes the way they think. "The trick degrades its user."
- Shreyas's correction #1 (sequencing): someone who internalizes phase one first stays more
  inoculated. They know what the idea looks like naked, so they can tell the costume apart from
  the body. Someone who learns the performance first never had the reference point — for them
  the framing *is* the idea.
- The inoculation is a practice, not a vaccine: phase-two rewards are immediate (the room
  applauds in real time); phase-one rewards (better decisions) are delayed, noisy, unattributed.
  Durable version: keep a venue where only plain statement counts.
- Shreyas's correction #2 (standing countermeasure): the first part isn't a stage, it's a
  continuous discipline running concurrently with the performing. "Try to sound less clever" —
  present tense, ongoing effort, no arrival. Part two creates the disease; part one is the
  standing treatment. The order of presentation tells you which is load-bearing.

### The seven cleverness moves (Claude's examples, endorsed by Shreyas)

1. **Premature pattern-matching** — "classic cold-start problem", "just Innovator's Dilemma".
   Compression ends the looking; the answer usually lives in a detail the framework doesn't
   predict.
2. **Elegant abstraction one level up** — "own the workflow, not the feature". Altitude avoids
   ground-level decisions. Cash-out test: what would we build differently next quarter?
3. **Contrarian inversion** — "churn isn't a retention problem, it's an acquisition problem".
   The syntactic shape of insight; inversion generated first, justification after.
4. **Aphorism as trump card** — "if you're not embarrassed by v1, you shipped too late".
   Borrowed authority as conversation-ender.
5. **Meta-escalation** — "we're solving the wrong problem". Costs nothing, unfalsifiable in the
   room, repositions the speaker above the work. Plain version carries the burden: "the problem
   is actually X, here's what I'd check."
6. **Coining a term** — "engagement debt", "expectations gap". A label is not a mechanism;
   the coinage hides how speculative the claim is.
7. **Second-order theater** — speculative causal chains that can't be checked live. Plain
   version: "Might annoy power users. Don't know. We could ask eight of them."

**Common structure:** cleverness produces the sensation of insight without paying the cost of
contact with specifics. The plain version is checkable, attributable, and might be visibly
wrong — which is exactly why it operates at the level of truth.

### The post's formal trick

The post performs its own structure: the first part is written plainly (short declarative
sentences); the last paragraph switches register — one long winding sentence, "strategically
abstruse," "whereupon" — it *is* the clever presentation it describes. And it does to the
reader what it advises doing to the room: decoding the irony delivers a jolt of feeling clever,
making the reader the approving executive in the sentence they're reading.
