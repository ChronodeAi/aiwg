# The Rulebook — Weston's 45 rules, 19 fallacies, and 3 definition rules

Disclosed reference for [`rulebook-for-arguments`](SKILL.md). Cite rules by number in any output.

An argument is not a verbal fight. It is a set of reasons offered in support of a conclusion — a means of inquiry for finding which views have the strongest support. Not all views are equal. The two failure modes: asserting conclusions without reasons, and substituting emotional language for evidence.

## Part I — Short arguments (R1–R6)

**R1 — Identify premises and conclusion.** Every argument has a conclusion (what you're proving) and premises (the reasons). Know which is which before anything else. Indicator words: therefore, so, because, since, thus.

**R2 — Develop ideas in a natural order.** Either conclusion first then reasons, or premises building to the conclusion at the end. Never reverse or bury the logic without purpose.

**R3 — Start from reliable premises.** Valid reasoning from weak premises still yields a weak conclusion. Every premise must be a well-established fact, a reasonable assumption, or defended by its own argument.

**R4 — Be concrete and concise.** Abstract and vague terms weaken arguments. Cut padding. One clear claim beats three fuzzy ones.

**R5 — Build on substance, not overtone.** "Ruthless corporate greed" is a label, not a reason. Language should clarify, not manipulate. The most violated rule in public discourse.

**R6 — Use consistent terms.** Switching terms for one concept breaks the logical connection. Equivocation lurks here.

## Part II — Generalizations (R7–R11)

Inductive: strong, never certain.

**R7 — Use more than one example.** A single example provides almost no support.

**R8 — Use representative examples.** Many examples still fail if drawn from one group, city, or era. Look for a genuine cross-section.

**R9 — Background rates may be crucial.** Ask hits ÷ total tries, not just how often something happened. Four tells that a base rate is missing:

- **Vivid hit** — one perfect result shown; ask how many attempts preceded it.
- **Horoscope loop** — one prediction came true; ask how many were made.
- **Bermuda Triangle** — dozens of incidents sounds alarming until you count the safe passages.
- **Doubling deception** — "thefts doubled" may mean 1 to 2. Percentages need base numbers.

**R10 — Statistics need a critical eye.** How was it measured, by whom, is it over-precise, is it extrapolated, is the comparison group right?

**R11 — Consider counterexamples.** Seek examples that contradict your generalization. Then either limit the claim or explain why they don't apply. Ignoring them is dishonesty.

## Part III — Analogy (R12)

**R12 — Analogies require relevantly similar examples.** The two things need not be identical, only similar in ways that bear on the conclusion. Irrelevant differences don't weaken an analogy; relevant ones do. Evaluate by listing key similarities and relevant differences.

## Part IV — Sources (R13–R17)

**R13 — Cite your sources.** An uncited statistic is an assertion, not evidence.

**R14 — Seek informed sources.** Credentials must match the domain of the claim.

**R15 — Seek impartial sources.** Prefer sources with no stake in the outcome. Look for independent corroboration.

**R16 — Cross-check sources.** Where equally qualified authorities disagree, acknowledge the division rather than appealing to consensus.

**R17 — Use the web with care.** Rely only on identifiable, independently reputable sources.

## Part V — Causes (R18–R21)

**R18 — Causal arguments start with correlations.** Correlation is necessary but not sufficient for causation.

**R19 — Correlations may have alternative explanations.** Four possibilities every time: A causes B; B causes A; a third factor C causes both; coincidence.

**R20 — Work toward the most likely explanation.** Which fits other known facts, which needs fewer assumptions? State uncertainty honestly.

**R21 — Expect complexity.** Real causes are multi-factorial, interacting, mutually reinforcing. Resist single-cause explanations; assign relative weights.

## Part VI — Deductive forms (R22–R28)

A *valid* argument is one where true premises force a true conclusion — a structural property, assessable without knowing whether the premises are true. A *sound* argument is valid with true premises.

**R22 — Modus ponens.** If P then Q. P. ∴ Q.

**R23 — Modus tollens.** If P then Q. Not-Q. ∴ Not-P. (Holmes: if the visitor were a stranger the dog would have barked; the dog didn't bark.)

**R24 — Hypothetical syllogism.** If P then Q. If Q then R. ∴ If P then R.

**R25 — Disjunctive syllogism.** P or Q. Not-P. ∴ Q. Verify the "or" is genuinely exclusive.

**R26 — Dilemma.** P or Q. If P then R. If Q then S. ∴ R or S.

**R27 — Reductio ad absurdum.** To prove P, assume not-P and show it leads to contradiction.

**R28 — Several steps.** Chain the forms; each conclusion becomes the next premise. Map the chain explicitly.

## Part VII — Extended arguments (R29–R33)

**R29 — Explore the issue.** Read widely, understand the strongest version of every position, know what has already been conceded and refuted.

**R30 — Spell out basic ideas as arguments.** Frame the core as an explicit 3–5 premise argument. It becomes the skeleton of everything else.

**R31 — Defend basic premises with arguments of their own.** Any premise a reasonable person might doubt needs its own support. The extended argument is a network of short ones.

**R32 — Consider objections.** Present the strongest objections in their best form, then answer them.

**R33 — Consider alternatives.** Showing a problem exists is insufficient; show your answer beats the other plausible ones. This may force revising the conclusion.

## Part VIII — Argumentative essays (R34–R39)

**R34 — Jump right in.** No "throughout history". State the issue and your position immediately.

**R35 — Make a definite claim or proposal.** "Something should be done about X" is not a thesis. "X should be done by Y because Z" is.

**R36 — Your argument is your outline.** After a one-paragraph summary, each section advances and defends one premise. Don't pad with background supporting nothing.

**R37 — Detail objections and meet them.** Place them after establishing your case, not before.

**R38 — Get feedback and use it.** Blind spots are invisible to the author by definition.

**R39 — Modesty, please.** Conclude only what the argument establishes. If you've shown X is likely, don't claim certainty.

## Part IX — Oral arguments (R40–R45)

**R40 — Reach out to your audience.** Tailor examples, vocabulary, and technicality to whoever is actually in the room.

**R41 — Be fully present.** Engage, don't recite. Respond to the room.

**R42 — Signpost your argument.** Oral arguments need more explicit signposting than written ones.

**R43 — Offer something positive.** Lead with what you are for. Purely critical arguments read as reactive.

**R44 — Use visual aids sparingly.** Only where they genuinely clarify. A bad chart is worse than no chart.

**R45 — End in style.** Return to the core claim, show what's established, leave something memorable.

## The 19 common fallacies

| Fallacy | Definition | How to spot it |
|---|---|---|
| **Ad hominem** | Attacking the person rather than the argument | "You can't trust X's argument because X is [attribute]" |
| **Ad ignorantiam** | True because unrefuted | "No one has disproved it, therefore it's true" |
| **Ad misericordiam** | A sob story as an argument for special treatment | Emotional appeal substituted for reason |
| **Ad populum** | Appeal to crowd or popularity | "Everyone believes X, so X is true" |
| **Affirming the consequent** | If P then Q; Q; ∴ P — invalid | Overlooks that Q may have other causes |
| **Begging the question** | Using the conclusion as a premise | The argument assumes what it must prove |
| **Circular argument** | Conclusion restates the premise | Starts and ends in the same place |
| **Complex question** | Framed so any answer affirms a hidden premise | "Have you stopped cheating?" |
| **Denying the antecedent** | If P then Q; not-P; ∴ not-Q — invalid | Overlooks that Q may hold for other reasons |
| **Equivocation** | One word, two meanings, same argument | The key term shifts between premise and conclusion |
| **False cause** | A questionable causal conclusion | Any error from R18–R21 |
| **False dilemma** | Two options where more exist | "Love it or leave it" |
| **Loaded language** | Emotion doing the work of evidence | Violates R5 |
| **Non sequitur** | Conclusion doesn't follow | Premises are irrelevant to the conclusion |
| **Overgeneralizing** | Too few or unrepresentative examples | Violates R7–R8 |
| **Overlooking alternatives** | One explanation assumed among many | Classic in causal reasoning (R19) |
| **Persuasive definition** | Loaded definition wins by assumption | "Evolution is the atheistic view" |
| **Red herring** | An irrelevant topic introduced as distraction | The new topic doesn't bear on the question |
| **Straw man** | Misrepresenting a view to attack it | Refuting a position nobody holds |

## Definitions (D1–D3)

**D1 — When terms are unclear, get specific.** Find the core meaning, then sharpen it for this context. Abstract double-duty terms ("freedom", "justice") need explicit scoping.

**D2 — When terms are contested, work from clear cases.** A good definition includes what clearly fits, excludes what clearly doesn't, draws the most defensible line through contested cases, and explains why that line makes sense.

**D3 — Definitions don't replace arguments.** Defining coffee as a drug doesn't settle whether to regulate it as one. Definitions clarify the terrain; arguments are still required.
