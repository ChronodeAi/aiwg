# Interventions by dominant blocker

Look up the dominant blocker named in step 4 of `SKILL.md`. Emit the named intervention's verbatim language.

## Push weak

The buyer has not felt the struggle hard enough. Pitching will fail.

- **Name the cost of doing nothing.** *"What happens if you don't fix this in the next six months?"* Let them answer in their own words.
- **Story their future self.** *"A year from now, if nothing changes, what does this look like?"*
- **Itemise the current state.** Walk through the repair bill on their current car, the support tickets they're absorbing, the hours they're losing — make the cost concrete. Do not name your product yet.
- **Default move:** stay in interview mode. Push-weak buyers are rarely deciding; they're in first-thought or passive looking. Run more [interviewing.md](interviewing.md).

## Pull weak

The buyer feels the struggle but can't see the better life.

- **Three-option contrast.** Present three meaningfully different options — not three flavors of the same option. The classic Moesta example: SUV / minivan / loaded compact SUV. The buyer eliminates one, then compares the remaining two against the rejected one. This surfaces the value code without asking for it.
- **Borrow social proof.** *"The buyers we work with most often use it like X. Some use it for Y. A few do Z."* Let the buyer self-select.
- **"So I can…" prompt.** *"If this worked perfectly, what would change in your week?"* Capture the answer verbatim — it becomes the SO I CAN slot in the job statement.

## Anxiety high

The dominant force in most stalled deals. The money is here.

- **Concierge the logistics.** Name the anxiety out loud and offer to solve it. *"Sounds like the hardest part is moving the data over. What if we did that for you in week one?"*
- **Play it out (60–90 day forward sim).** *"Let's walk through what the next 90 days look like. Day one you do X. Week two you'll probably hit Y. By day 60 we'd expect Z."* The point is not the plan — it's that anxiety dies when the future stops being a void.
- **Pre-mortem the regret.** *"What would have to be true 90 days from now for you to say this was a mistake?"* Then address those conditions directly.
- **Lower the cost of being wrong.** A pilot, a 30-day out, a phased rollout. Anxiety is a function of *cost-of-wrong*, not probability-of-wrong.

## Habit high

The buyer is attached to the present. Habit only loses to a forcing event.

- **Find or build a time wall.** Find first — search the buyer's calendar for an existing deadline (lease end, fiscal year, audit, holiday, project launch). If none exists, *build* one only if it's real: an expiring trial, a price increase the buyer knows is genuine, a slot in your delivery schedule that's actually limited. A manufactured time wall the buyer sniffs out destroys trust.
- **Make the cost of staying visible.** Itemise the workarounds the buyer has built up. The point is not "your tools are bad" — it's "look how much energy you're spending on glue."
- **Three-option contrast again.** Forcing the buyer to compare alternatives breaks the gravitational hold of the present.

## Onboarding / ongoing (post-sale)

Even after the switch, two follow-on moves prevent churn:

- **Watch the big hire.** First use must match the expectations poured in concrete during *deciding*. If you over-promised, recover before the gap widens.
- **Watch the little hires.** Daily use is where the habit forms. If the product gets bypassed twice, intervene — a check-in, a tutorial, a workflow tweak. Habits ossify in roughly 30 days.

## Universal rules

- One option fails; two options are a tie; **three options force a tradeoff**.
- Reduce anxiety before increasing pull — anxiety reduction has a higher elasticity per unit effort.
- A time wall must be real or the buyer will detect the manipulation.
- "Play it out" is not a sales script — it's a calibration of expectations. Do it honestly or skip it.
