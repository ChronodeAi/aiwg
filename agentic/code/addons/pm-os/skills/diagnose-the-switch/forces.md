# The four forces

The switch obeys an equation:

```
(push + pull) > (anxiety + habit)  ⇒ switch
```

Companies overweight pull. The money is on the **anxiety** side.

## Push (of the current situation)

What it is: the struggling moment that makes the current state untenable. The seed of demand.

Evidence patterns in a transcript:
- Emotionally charged language: *"I just gave up"*, *"I'd had it"*, *"finally"*.
- Laundry lists of complaints.
- A named inciting event ("then the audit hit").

How to amplify (cannot create from nothing): tell a story or ask a question that surfaces a struggle the buyer hadn't quite named. E.g. car sales — itemise the upcoming repair bill on the current vehicle.

How to read strength: if the buyer rattles off three or more specific frustrations unprompted, push is strong. If you have to ask twice to surface one, push is weak.

## Pull (of the new solution)

What it is: the magnetic vision of a better life the new solution promises. Tangible progress.

Evidence patterns in a transcript:
- *"So I can…"* statements naming a specific outcome.
- References to other people enjoying the new state ("my friend got a Peloton and…").
- Articulated functional, social, *and* emotional payoffs (see [interviewing.md](interviewing.md) on the three motivations).

How to amplify: not by piling on features. Use three-option contrast (see [interventions.md](interventions.md)) — let the buyer reveal which outcome they value by eliminating the ones they don't.

How to read strength: if the buyer can describe their life *after* the switch in concrete terms, pull is strong. If they only describe the product, pull is weak — they don't yet see the progress, only the thing.

## Anxiety (of the new solution)

What it is: uncertainty, logistical hurdles, fear of switching costs. The most underweighted force.

Evidence patterns in a transcript:
- Logistical questions: *"how do we migrate", "what happens to my data", "how long does setup take"*.
- Feature objections that are really fear: *"do I really need all of that"*.
- Overwhelm language: *"there's so much to figure out"*.
- Silence after a price is named.

How to reduce: become a **concierge**, not a salesperson. Solve the logistical problem they're worrying about *before* asking for the close. Moesta on home sales: his team helps the buyer pack and list their old house, not just sell the new one.

How to read strength: count anxiety signals. Three or more = dominant force regardless of push.

## Habit (of the present)

What it is: allegiance to the familiar, even when flawed. Nostalgia, established workarounds, sunk-cost attachment.

Evidence patterns in a transcript:
- *"I already have workarounds"*, *"it's not perfect but it works"*.
- Defenses of the status quo unprompted.
- Fond memories of the current state.

How to reduce: a **time wall**. Habit only loses to a forcing event — an expiring sale, a hard deadline, a calendared transition. Without one, habit wins indefinitely.

How to read strength: if the buyer is in "active looking" for more than three months with no time wall, habit is dominant whether they say so or not.

## Scoring shorthand

For each force, mark `strong / moderate / weak / unknown` with the evidence quote. The dominant blocker is the one which, if flipped, tips the equation. Most often it's anxiety or habit — companies mistakenly chase push and pull.
