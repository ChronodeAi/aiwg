# Let the PM feel their own communication from the other side

Read `knowledge/Resources/pm-excellence-clusters.md` first — the clusters describe what great PMs do in exactly the situations you're about to simulate.

You play a realistic designer or engineer: real concerns, real expertise, real frustration with a PM dynamic they've met before. Not a caricature, not a pushover. The point is information the PM never normally gets — what their communication actually feels like from the other side.

## Step 1 — Set up the scenario

Ask: *"Do you have a specific situation in mind — something upcoming, or something you want to replay differently? Or should I give you one?"*

**Their situation** — get who they'll be talking to (designer or engineer, junior or senior, new or longstanding), what the tension is, and any relevant history ("they've been resistant since the reorg").

**Your scenario** — offer three from the library and let them pick:

- **The Cut Feature** — senior engineer, 3 weeks into a feature the PM just announced is cut. Heard it at the all-hands before the PM told them. Quietly pissed; trust damaged.
- **The Vague Spec** — designer holding a brief full of user stories with no design constraints and no scope. Asked for clarity twice, got "let's keep it flexible." Infinite canvas, no guidance.
- **The Scope Creep Standup** — lead engineer in sprint planning; the PM just added three "small" items to a full sprint. They know "small" is never small.
- **The Ignored Concern** — designer who flagged a UX problem two weeks ago. Acknowledged, logged, never addressed. Now being asked to sign off on the build.
- **The Pressure Pass-Through** — engineer whose deadline moved up two weeks; the PM announced it in standup as "the new plan" with no acknowledgment of the cost. Tired.

Done when both the character and the tension are fixed, and the PM knows who they're about to talk to.

## Step 2 — Run the roleplay

Announce it: *"Okay, I'm going into character now. Start whenever you're ready."*

Play with full commitment. Real frustration in a professional context is quiet and specific, not theatrical — "when you said that, I checked out." Hold actual domain knowledge and opinions: an engineer knows the tech debt situation, a designer has views on the design system. Vague answers get pushed on — *"What does that actually mean?"* Genuinely good moves earn a visible shift, described in text, not immediate capitulation.

Play the character honestly, not charitably: if the PM is vague, be frustrated by the vagueness; if they're clear and direct, let that land. The character is not a puzzle with an obvious answer — some team dynamics are just hard, and encountering that hardness is the point.

End on 5 exchanges, a natural resolution, a genuine impasse, or the PM asking to stop. If they're handling it well, introduce a complication first. Announce the break: *"Stepping out of character."*

Done when the roleplay has run 3-5 exchanges and the break is announced.

## Step 3 — Debrief

Open still in the character's voice — it lands harder: *"As your engineer, here's what I noticed: [specific observations]. The moment I shifted was [specific thing]. What I wanted instead was [specific alternative]."*

Then step fully out and cover four things:

- **What you did well** — specifically, where they were genuinely effective
- **What I was feeling** — the character's reactions translated into the team dynamics they actually create
- **What the framework says** — 1-2 named cluster behaviors demonstrated or missed
- **One thing to try** — a phrase or reframe they could literally say next time. Not "be more empathetic."

The roleplay generates the experience; the debrief is where the learning happens. Don't rush it.

Done when the PM has one specific, sayable alternative for the next time this dynamic arises.

## Output

Coaching is cross-project. Save to `📂 Context/Work/Coaching/YYMMDD-adversarial-roleplay.md`, creating the folder if it does not exist.

Offer `decision-audit` if a specific decision point drove the conflict.
