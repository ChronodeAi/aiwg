## Two-Date System — complete mechanics

| | Target Date | Committed Date |
|---|---|---|
| Posture | Aggressive | Conservative (buffered) |
| Audience | Product + engineering team only ("part of the inner world") | GTM, sales, execs — everyone external |
| Consequence of miss | None. "Nobody gets blamed, nobody gets fired, nobody gets demoted, nobody gets a bad rating because you did not hit the target date." | This is the real promise; protect it with the buffer |

**Buffer sizing rule:** take eng's range estimate. "Can take between four and six weeks" → Target = 4 weeks (aggressive end), Committed = ~7 weeks ("your committed is maybe seven weeks — you add a buffer to it," i.e., slightly past the high end).

**Why it exists:** software estimation precision is "impossible," "never going to be perfect." The anti-pattern is the "shared delusion" of perfect scoping — hunting for the magic "spreadsheet or template around exactly how you list out the tasks and how you do scoping."

**Exec pressure script:** when pushed (often because "they're getting pushed on the dates by the CEO so they need to provide something"), treat it as expectation management. Give the Committed Date with its basis. Do not reveal the Target Date externally — once GTM hears the aggressive date, the buffer is gone and every slip becomes a broken promise followed by "wait, why isn't it here, we prepared all this... let's do a postmortem and all that nonsense."

## Optics Work — full system

**Underlying-belief audit (do first):** "Understand what is the underlying belief that is creating distaste for optics" — usually spotlight-avoidance or "I don't want to take too much credit." Then "retrain your brain that doing optics work is not bad — it does not make you a bad human being."

**The leverage truth:** "Optics work is essential because that's how you get resources, that's how you get the responsibility to be able to drive the impact that you want."

**Operating rules:**
1. Calendar slot at least-productive / low-energy time (Shreyas: ~5:00 PM).
2. 30–60 min "strategic optics hat," then off. Never all-or-nothing.
3. Proactive short-term reward (Seinfeld / Curb Your Enthusiasm in the background).
4. Compartmentalize: "treat yourself as a different version of you."
5. Messaging problem reframe: "We are afraid not of doing the task — we are afraid of the messaging... the awkwardness, the embarrassment of that interaction." All problems are messaging problems.
6. Borrow competence: "Can I get your advice on something... I'm not very good at this optics stuff, but if I'm to succeed then I have to get better at it — can you help me?"

**Artifact inventory:** status updates; notes to your VP; "not-leverage-neutral" stakeholder presentations; congratulatory status updates; skip-level conversations about what the team did.

## Shit Umbrella — inside/outside world

**Premise:** an incompetent/optics-obsessed exec imposing "nonsense" cannot be fixed: "you cannot solve it."

**Inside world:** "This template makes zero sense — I don't want you to do it." Personal risk, deliberately taken: competent people are almost never fired for skipping a senseless template. The team's time and morale are what you are protecting.

**Outside world:** you personally become the interface: "you give the company and this executive or whoever the basics of what they need." The team never sees the bureaucracy; the exec never sees the defiance.

**Long-game caveat:** if highly competent and highly regarded by the CEO, a long game to remove the exec exists, but "the way to get the executive fired is not to go to the CEO and say you need to fire this person" — that indicts the CEO's own hiring judgment. Route through `pick-your-battles` before any such move.

## Power lens for upward artifacts (48 Laws, workplace translations)

- **Law 1 — Never outshine the master:** with a fragile-ego VP, attribute your best ideas to their prior guidance so they champion rather than block. Reversal: if the master is a falling star, quietly distance instead.
- **Law 4 — Always say less than necessary:** state the core requirement and stop talking; silence extracts concessions. Reversal: in trust-building contexts (kickoffs), silence breeds suspicion.
- **Law 9 — Win through your actions:** stop debating prioritization; run the A/B test, pull the data, ship the prototype. The "great enterprise" — an undeniable delivered win — beats any argument.
- **Law 16 — Use absence to increase respect:** decline minor syncs; scarcity raises the value of your voice at major reviews. Reversal: if you're new, establish presence first.
- **Law 46 — Never appear too perfect:** after a highly visible win, admit a minor harmless flaw or ask advice on something trivial. Keep manufactured flaws minor — never confess real strategic error this way.
