# Manage the Upward Channel

**Purpose:** Control what is promised and what is seen. Three branches: date commitments, optics work, and containment of imposed nonsense.

**Agency boundary:** Advise and draft only. Drafted updates and date communications go to the user for sending; never deliver a commitment to anyone on the user's behalf.

## Input/Output Contract

**Accepts:** An upward deliverable: a date request, an update/presentation to write, or an imposed process/template.
**Produces:** (1) a two-date plan with buffer and audience map, or (2) a finished optics artifact plus a standing optics block, or (3) an inside/outside containment setup.
**Passes to:** `pick-your-battles` only if the user is contemplating open resistance to an exec. Otherwise terminal.

## Branch 1 — Commit to a date

**The one mistake to avoid:** the "shared delusion" that precise estimation is possible — believing "the entire world is missing some spreadsheet or template around exactly how you list out the tasks." Estimation is an "imprecise science"; stop "banging your head against the wall" and manage expectations instead.

**The Two-Date System** (full mechanics in `references/mechanics.md`):
1. **Target Date** — aggressive, internal to the product/eng team only. "Nobody gets blamed, nobody gets fired, nobody gets demoted, nobody gets a bad rating because you did not hit the target date."
2. **Committed Date** — conservative, buffered. "The committed date is what the go-to-market teams go by, what the sales team goes by." It is the only date that leaves the room.
3. **Buffer sizing:** if eng estimates 4–6 weeks, Target = 4 weeks; Committed = ~7 weeks (slightly past the high end).
4. **When execs push for earlier:** do not invent a magic spreadsheet under pressure ("they're getting pushed on the dates by the CEO so they need to provide something"). Hold the frame: this is expectation management, not estimation engineering.

Power lens (Law 4): when delivering the date, say less than necessary. State the Committed Date and the confidence basis; do not volunteer the Target Date or the internal math — every extra detail is a hook for renegotiation.

Done when a Target Date and a buffered Committed Date are both set, and only the Committed Date is anything external ever hears.

## Branch 2 — The optics block

**Mindset first:** the belief "optics is distasteful and I should just do good work and let everything else take care of itself" is the failure. "Optics work is essential because that's how you get resources, that's how you get the responsibility to be able to drive the impact that you want."

**The system:**
- **Schedule it at your least productive time** — "when you're feeling low energy," e.g. 5:00 PM.
- **Time-box it:** "Don't go with the all-or-nothing model." Wear the "strategic optics hat" for 30–60 minutes a day, then take it off.
- **Pair a reward:** "You'd be surprised how much optics work you can get done with one hour of Seinfeld running in the background."
- **Compartmentalize:** "Treat yourself as a different version of you" while doing it.
- **Messaging fear is the real blocker:** "We are afraid not of doing the task — we are afraid of the messaging, the awkwardness, the embarrassment of that interaction." All problems are messaging problems; if stuck, ask the peer who's great at it: "I'm not very good at this optics stuff, but if I'm to succeed I have to get better — can you help me?"

**What counts:** status updates, notes to your VP, stakeholder-keeping presentations, congratulatory updates, telling your skip-level what the team did.

Power lens: prefer the great-enterprise route — let an undeniable result be the message (Law 9: "win through your actions, never through argument"), then make sure the result is *seen* (the optics block is how). Law 1: in updates to a fragile-ego exec, attribute direction to their guidance. Law 46: after a big visible win, admit a minor harmless flaw — "never appear too perfect."

Done when the artifact is drafted and a standing time-boxed slot for future optics work is set.

## Branch 3 — Contain an imposed process (the shit umbrella)

When an incompetent or optics-obsessed exec imposes a "shit planning process" or "horrible planning template": **you cannot fix them.** "Create two different worlds."

- **Inside world:** take personal risk as a true owner. Tell your team: "this template makes zero sense — I don't want you to do it." Competent people are almost never fired for skipping a senseless template.
- **Outside world:** become the interface yourself. Personally "give the company and this executive the basics of what they need" so the team never touches the bureaucracy.
- **Long game (rare):** getting an exec removed is never done by telling the CEO "you need to fire this person" — that tells the CEO their hiring judgment was wrong. If you are contemplating moves against an exec, stop and run `pick-your-battles`.

Done when the inside-world message to the team and the outside-world interface plan are both stated.

## Output format

```
BRANCH: [date / optics / containment]
PLAN: [two-date table with audience map | drafted artifact + standing block setup | inside/outside split]
SAY EXACTLY: [the committed-date sentence / the update's framing lines / the team-facing line]
DO NOT: [reveal target date externally / skip the reward pairing / fight the template in public]
```

## Failure modes

- **One date for everyone.** GTM plans against your aggressive date; delay becomes "let's do a postmortem and all that nonsense — bullshit waste of time."
- **Optics debt.** Skipping updates because they feel slimy, until resourcing decisions get made without you in the picture.
- **Umbrella inversion:** passing the template down to the team while privately grumbling — the exact opposite of the job.
