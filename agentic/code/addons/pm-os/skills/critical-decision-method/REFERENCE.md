# CDM Reference

## Quality Bar — What Good Looks Like

Use these as binary checks (Pass/Fail) when reviewing a completed CDM session:

| Check | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| **"Aha" moment** | Subject has a moment of realizing *how* they knew what they previously called intuition |
| **Diagrammable timeline** | The story can be drawn as a situation-awareness timeline — events in order, with what the expert knew at each stage |
| **Specific perceptual cues** | At least one critical cue is specific (a smell, a number, a word someone said, a pause, an absence) — not vague ("experience", "gut") |
| **Negative cues present** | At least one "negative cue" identified — something that didn't happen that should have, and what its absence meant |
| **Actionable novice contrast** | Pass 4 names at least one specific trap a beginner would fall into and *why* they'd fall into it |

**Diagrammability test:** After the interview, ask: can I draw this as a timeline with labeled decision points and what the expert knew at each? If not, the interview lacked enough timeline work (Pass 2) or enough cue-surfacing (Pass 3). Return to those passes.

**Red flag:** If the final output contains only general takeaways ("be more careful," "trust your experience") with no specific perceptual triggers, the interview has failed. Cues must be specific enough that a novice could be trained to notice them.

## Probing Question Bank

### Pass 2 — Timeline Probes
- "When exactly did you first sense something was off?"
- "What were you expecting to happen at that point — and what actually happened?"
- "Draw me a timeline: what's the sequence from the moment you entered the situation to when you committed to a course of action?"
- "At what point did you know? What changed?"

### Pass 3 — Decision Point Probes

**Cue elicitation:**
- "What specifically caught your attention?"
- "What told you this was [X type of situation]?"
- "What were you looking for that you didn't find?"
- "Was there something that should have been there that wasn't?"

**Alternative consideration:**
- "What other options were on the table at that moment?"
- "Did you consider [alternative]? Why not?"
- "What would have had to be different for you to choose a different course?"

**Hypothetical (most powerful):**
- "If [key cue] had not been present — what would you have done instead?"
- "Imagine [pivotal moment] didn't happen. Why might that be? What would it mean?"
- "What's the minimum information you needed to act the way you did? What if you'd only had half of it?"
- "At [decision point], what would have made you hesitate?"

**Assumption surfacing:**
- "What were you assuming that you didn't say out loud?"
- "What did you take for granted that a newcomer wouldn't know to take for granted?"

### Pass 4 — Novice Check Probes
- "If I were the one making this decision, what mistakes would I make? Why would I make them?"
- "What would a first-year [role] have gotten wrong here?"
- "What's the thing that looks obvious to you but wouldn't to someone with 6 months of experience?"
- "What do you know now that you didn't know 5 years ago that made the difference in this decision?"

## Theory: The RPD Model

CDM is built on the Recognition-Primed Decision model. Experts under time pressure don't compare options — they:

1. **Recognize** the situation as typical/atypical based on pattern matching
2. **Generate** a single workable course of action (not the optimal one — the first satisficing one)
3. **Simulate** the action mentally to check if it will work
4. If it fails the simulation, move to the next option; if it passes, execute it

This means:
- Asking "what options did you consider?" often produces nothing useful — experts didn't compare
- The real question is "what made this situation look like [type]?" — the recognition step
- The second real question is "what would have made it look like something else?" — the hypothetical step

## What Makes a Good CDM Story

**Good story signals:**
- Expert says "I just knew" or "something felt off" → rich source of implicit cues
- Expert says "anyone would have done the same" → probe harder; this often hides deep expertise
- The incident is one they tell others at work → high judgment content
- The story involves a moment where the expert's read changed suddenly

**Redirect signals (story needs replacement):**
- Purely procedural ("I followed the protocol")
- Dramatic but decision-poor ("it was chaos but everyone knew what to do")
- Too far in the past to recall decision-level detail
- Expert is genuinely new to the domain (< 1 year, minimal pattern library)

## Landmark CDM Studies (for grounding and examples)

| Domain | Key finding | CDM application |
|---|---|---|
| Fireground commanders | Experts never compare options — they recognize + simulate | Revealed the RPD model |
| NICU nurses | "Intuition" about sepsis = specific pattern cues (half new to literature; some opposite to adult cues) | Critical cue list → training materials that reduced new nurse turnover |
| Chess masters | First instinct is high-quality; time pressure doesn't degrade experts | Validated RPD under extreme constraints |
| Military commanders | Expertise = reading adversary intent via mental simulation + communicating Commander's Intent | Built squad leader training programs; OJT for LA County Fire |
| AWACS radar operators | Decision requirements revealed interface forced wrong information format | Redesign improved threat detection |
| Retail OJT | Coaches couldn't teach perceptual skills because they hadn't been trained to | OJT program → $24K/month/store sales increase |
